How the tide keeps time

Twice a day the sea climbs the harbour wall and slips back again. The schedule it keeps is older than any clock in town.
By Ines Halvorsen · 9 min read

The harbourmaster in Porthlow keeps a chalkboard by the slipway. Every morning she writes the day's two high waters on it, to the minute, and every evening the sea arrives more or less when she said it would. Nobody in the village finds this remarkable. They should.

The tide is a wave, and a very long one. Its crest is half a world away from its trough, and it is pulled around the planet by the Moon and, more gently, by the Sun. What reaches the wall in Porthlow is the far edge of that wave, bent and slowed by every shelf and headland it crossed on the way in.

Fifty minutes late, every day

The Moon comes back to the same place in our sky about fifty minutes later each day, and the tide follows it. That is why the chalkboard never repeats: Monday's high water at six becomes Tuesday's at ten to seven, and by the end of the week the evening tide has slid into the night.

Fishermen learned this long before anyone could explain it. The old tide tables in the harbour office are hand-ruled, with columns for the Moon's age and the hour of high water, and the numbers in them are startlingly close to the ones the port authority prints today.

Springs and neaps

Twice a month, at new moon and full moon, the Sun and the Moon pull in a line. The tides run higher and lower than usual, and the harbour floor shows its old anchors and lost moorings. These are spring tides, though they have nothing to do with the season.

In between, when the Sun and Moon pull at right angles, the range shrinks. These neap tides barely cover the lower steps, and the boats that sit on the mud at springs float all day. A careful skipper plans a week around them.

Where the clock goes wrong

Wind can hold the water back or pile it up against the coast. A deep low-pressure system lifts the sea like a hand pressing less hard on a bath. On those days the chalkboard is wrong by twenty minutes and a foot of water, and the harbourmaster adds a note in red.

Rivers argue with the tide, too. Upstream of the bridge, the flood has to push against everything the hills have sent down, and after heavy rain the high water there arrives late and short, if it arrives at all.

Reading the wall

If you stand on the slipway for an hour you can watch it happen: a weed line darkening, a step going under, the moored boats swinging round to face the other way. The sea is not in a hurry, but it is never late by much.

The chalkboard will be there tomorrow, with two new times on it. Write them down, come back, and see how close she got.
